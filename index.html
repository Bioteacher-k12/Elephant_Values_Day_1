Rebuilding Gorongosa National Park

Field Video: Gorongosa Restoration

Watch the documentary clip below to observe how scientists and local residents are restoring Gorongosa.

Watch Gorongosa Video in Google Drive

Located in central Mozambique at the southern tip of the Great Rift Valley, Gorongosa National Park suffered a catastrophic collapse during decades of civil conflict and unregulated poaching—losing more than 90% of its large wildlife populations. Today, a long-term conservation partnership is rebuilding the park through active ecosystem restoration, wildlife reintroductions, and deep community partnerships that connect park health to human well-being.

Context Hint: Ecological & Community Pillars
  • Rebuilding Wildlife & Ecosystems: Recruiting and training local rangers to stop poaching, reintroducing herbivores and predators to restore food webs, and tracking biodiversity recovery with scientists.
  • Community Value: Creating sustainable tourism and park employment, funding local healthcare clinics and schools, and helping farmers grow shade-grown coffee that protects Mount Gorongosa’s rainforest.

Human-Elephant Conflict & Two-Lens Analysis

Shrinking Habitats & Agricultural Conflict

As farming, roadways, and towns expand across Africa and Asia, wild elephant habitats are increasingly broken into smaller, disconnected patches. Blocked from traditional seasonal routes and natural foraging zones, elephants frequently wander into nearby farms in search of food and water. A single adult elephant eats up to 450 kg (nearly 1,000 pounds) of vegetation each day and tramples roughly the same amount while moving through cultivated fields, meaning one herd can wipe out a family’s harvest overnight. This creates starkly different experiences: while viewers watching wildlife films from afar feel deep affection and admiration for elephants, rural residents living next to wildlife corridors often endure fear, sleepless nights defending crops, and severe financial hardship.

Impacts on Local Communities & Elephant Populations

Rural households and commercial farms bear heavy costs from these encounters. Smallholder families can lose an entire year’s food supply and income in a single raid, while commercial agriculture also suffers major damage—in Riau, Indonesia alone, elephant damage to oil palm and timber plantations costs an estimated $105 million per year. Direct confrontations can also turn deadly: elephants kill more than 100 people annually in India, and killed over 200 people in Kenya across a seven-year period. Faced with lost harvests and human fatalities, local residents and wildlife officials often resort to retaliatory killings, including authorities shooting 50 to 120 “problem” elephants each year in Kenya and fatal poisonings near plantations in Indonesia. Alongside habitat loss and ivory poaching, these conflicts have fueled steep century-long population declines: over the past 100 years, African elephants have dropped from 3–5 million down to 470,000–690,000, while Asian elephants have declined from roughly 100,000 down to 35,000–50,000.
